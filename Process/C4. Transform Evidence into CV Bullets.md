## Purpose
Transform the approved evidence from Step [[C2. Map JD Requirements to Supporting Evidence]] into concise, impactful CV bullet points. These bullets must remain truthful to the candidate’s actual experience while being written in language that improves both **ATS compatibility** and **Talent Acquisition appeal**.

The goal is to produce bullet points that:

- Clearly demonstrate fulfilment of the Job Description requirements.
- Use strong action verbs and, wherever possible, include measurable results or impact.
- Maintain a consistent, professional tone aligned with senior-level roles.

---

## A. Execution Sequence

1. Use the **final cleaned C2 export** as the single source of truth. Only use evidence that was marked as approved (Green / final set) in C2.
2. For each requirement, review the associated evidence (Reference + Original Text).
3. Transform each piece of evidence into one or more CV bullet points following [[C4. Transform Evidence into CV Bullets#B. CV Bullet Writing Principles|B. CV Bullet Writing Principles]]
4. When one requirement has multiple pieces of evidence, create separate bullets (or group them logically when appropriate).
5. Add the resulting bullet(s) in a new column called **CV Bullet**.
6. Review all bullets for consistency, impact, and truthful representation of the candidate’s experience.
7. Produce the final output table.

## B. CV Bullet Writing Principles

When transforming evidence into CV bullets, apply the following principles:

### 1. Truthfulness First

- When transforming evidence into bullets, use the `Original Text` column from the final  [[C2. Map JD Requirements to Supporting Evidence]] export as the primary source for the bullet content. This ensures truthfulness. 
- Use the `Requirement Skills` column to guide the language and focus of the **bullet** — what to foreground out of the evidence, and which of the candidate's words the reader of this posting is looking for. It does **not** guide the bracketed tag; see [[C4. Transform Evidence into CV Bullets#5. Skill Association – Consistent and Non-Forceful Approach|§B.5]].
- The `Reference` column indicates the exact source of the evidence in the Profile Reference Workbook.
- Avoid significantly rephrasing or generalising the evidence just to make it sound more impressive or to fit Job Description language.
- If the original evidence is specific, the bullet should reflect that specificity. If it is more general, do not artificially narrow or broaden it.
- Do not invent achievements, exaggerate scope, or add elements that are not supported by the source material.
- If the original evidence is indirect or partial, the bullet should still reflect reality (you may soften the claim rather than overstate it).


### 2. Natural Integration of Job Description Language

- Incorporate relevant keywords and phrasing from the Job Description **only when they fit naturally** within the bullet.
- Do not force Job Description keywords into the bullet. If incorporating them requires changing the meaning or flow of the original evidence, leave them out. The bracketed tag at the end is the appropriate place to reference relevant skills.
- The objective is **compatibility**, not keyword stuffing.
- The bullet must still read naturally and professionally.


### 3. Strong Action Verbs

- Start every bullet with a strong, specific action verb (e.g., _Led_, _Designed_, _Implemented_, _Coordinated_, _Built_, _Delivered_, _Transformed_).
- Avoid weak verbs such as _Helped_, _Assisted_, _Worked on_, or _Involved in_.

### 4. Results and Impact

- Include measurable results, scale, or business impact **when they exist** in the Original Text.
- Do not invent or exaggerate results that are not supported by the evidence.
- If no clear result exists in the evidence, focus on the **action and scope** instead of forcing a weak outcome.
- Examples of good additions: timelines, number of stakeholders, countries involved, efficiency gains, regulatory compliance achieved, cost savings, etc.
- Some rows are a recorded **STAR result** — the outcome itself, cited by a ref shaped `1-R3`. Their Original Text may end with `— measured: …`; that is the row's own recorded metric and it is as truthful as the sentence before it, so use the number. Where the metric names a figure the sentence does not (assets moved, capital, savings), that figure is still the candidate's own record — state it, do not round it up and do not restate it as something it isn't.

**The `context:` line, and writing an outcome as a bullet.**

A row may carry a `context:` line between `original:` and `my skills:`, reading `outcome of STAR <n>: <STAR title>`. It appears on STAR result rows and names the piece of work the outcome came out of.

It exists because a result on its own states a consequence with no actor — *"Gradual branch FTE reallocation from back-office to front-office over 6 years following SCE go-live"* is something that happened, not something anyone did, and §B.3 asks every bullet to open on a strong action verb. Use the `context:` line to supply that verb and its object, then close on the outcome the `original:` text actually records:

> **original:** Gradual branch FTE reallocation from back-office to front-office over 6 years following SCE go-live. — measured: 6 years transition
> **context:** outcome of STAR 1: Establishment of a Servicing Center in Portugal
> **bullet:** Established a Shared Services Centre in Portugal, enabling branch FTEs to be reallocated from back-office to front-office over the six years that followed go-live.

Two limits on this, both of them §B.1 restated:

- The `context:` line is a **STAR title**, not evidence. It tells you what the work was; it does not license any claim about scope, role or result that the `original:` text does not carry. Lead with it, then let the outcome speak.
- Where the `original:` text already opens on an action of its own (*"Delivered a fully operational Shared Services Centre within 1 year and 8 months…"*), the `context:` line has nothing to add. Leave it out rather than prefixing the bullet with the project name twice.


### 5. Skill Association – Consistent and Non-Forceful Approach

Use the following hierarchy when associating skills with bullets:

**Primary Method (Default):**  
Add skills as **bracketed tags** at the end of the bullet, named in the candidate's own CV register (see *Naming the tag* below). This is the most consistent and reliable method.

*Superseded (2026-08-24, [[C4 Writes CV-Grade Skill Tags]]): this line previously read "Use language that is close to the Job Lead's wording where possible." That instruction is why the CV printed "Work Autonomously" and "Team Player & Cooperation" — the step was obeying it. The Job Lead's wording keeps its own column (`requirement_skills`, B2's extraction) and its own job (ATS matching at C2); it is not what prints.*

**Secondary Method (Selective Use):**  
Apply **light, selective bold** inline **only** when the skill can be highlighted naturally without requiring changes to the original evidence. 

Do not attempt to force bold inline integration. If the bullet needs to be significantly rephrased to include a skill in bold, use a bracketed tag instead. The goal is to improve human readability, not to artificially insert keywords.

**Rule:** Avoid heavily mixing both methods (bold + bracketed tags) within the same role or section, unless it genuinely improves clarity. Prioritise consistency and readability.

**Example of good application:**

> Acted as a **trusted mediator** between the Management Board and department heads during a politically sensitive transformation, enabling progress despite internal resistance. _(Senior Stakeholder Management, Conflict Mediation)_

*The tags are the register at work: "Senior" states the level the mediation was done at, and "Conflict Mediation" is the compound re-expression of the curated competence `Conflict Resolution / Mediation: Acting as Mediator` — not the posting's phrasing, and not the table's wording either.*

**Note on Skill Integration:**
The preferred approach is to use bracketed tags as the main method for linking bullets to skills. Selective bold should only be used sparingly and naturally. The objective is to produce authentic, readable bullets that still support ATS matching through relevant keywords in the bracketed tags.

**Naming the tag — the register**

The tag is what the CV's **Skills section** prints. It is therefore written for the reader of the CV, in the candidate's own professional register — not in the Job Description's. A Job Description is a population document: it mixes skills, competences and attributes, and it is pitched below the level a senior CV is written at. Borrowing its wording drags the Skills section down with it.

**Write in the register of `skills_master`.** You are given that table — the candidate's own names for his own capabilities, with the alternate wordings each is known by. Read it for the *level and shape* the names have, and write at that level.

**It is an exemplar, not a list to choose from.** Use an entry verbatim when it genuinely fits the bullet. When none fits, **coin a name in the same register** — that is expected, not a fallback. Half of the entries in the candidate's own hand-built CVs ("Board-Grade Synthesis", "Neutral Sounding Board", "Governance Operating Rhythm") appear in no table at all. What you must never do is stretch a claim to reach an entry: the tag has to be earned by the bullet it sits on.

**The row's `My Skills` is the capability the tag is about.** It was selected at [[C2. Map JD Requirements to Supporting Evidence]] from the candidate's curated tables, and it may be a competence or an attribute rather than a skill — a posting asking for discretion, or resilience under scrutiny, is genuinely answered by an attribute. Do not echo it verbatim and do not drop it: **re-express it** at CV grade. The candidate's own CV is the model — `Confidentiality & Trust` printed as "Confidentiality & Discretion"; `Resilience` + `Tolerance for Stress` printed as "Resilience & Composure Under Pressure"; `Good Listener` + `Mediator` printed as "Neutral Sounding Board".

**Five rules, all of them visible in the hand-built benchmark:**

1. **Consolidate, do not enumerate.** One compound entry per capability, covering the whole of it — not several near-duplicate facets. *Cost Allocation · Cost Transformation · Cost Optimization · Cost Benefit Management* is four tags where the CV wants one: **Transfer Pricing & Cost Optimization**. Joining is also the stronger ATS position, not the weaker one — the compound carries three matchable terms where the atom carries one.
2. **State the level.** Name the seniority, scale or scope the capability was exercised at: **Senior** Stakeholder Management, **Large-Scale** Transformation Leadership, **Multi-Country / Cross-Cultural** Leadership, **Board-Level** Partnering. This is what makes the section read as a director's CV rather than a competency inventory.
3. **Anchor parenthetically where it adds precision.** "Corporate Governance & Regulatory Compliance **(EBA)**", "Stakeholder Influence **(Influence Without Authority)**". Only where the anchor is true of the evidence and genuinely sharpens the claim — never as decoration.
4. **No table-stakes tooling.** "MS Office Proficiency" and its like are assumed at this level and cost a slot.
5. **No phrase lifted whole from the posting, and no languages.** "Work Autonomously" and "Team Player & Cooperation" are the posting talking. Languages belong to the CV's Languages section and never appear here ([[C5. Build and Manage the Skills Section]] §B.4).

**Consistency across bullets.** Tags must still read as one voice: consistent noun-phrase style throughout, no mixing of a bare two-word tag with a sentence-like one, and no two tags that are near-spellings of each other — those become duplicate entries in the Skills list. Length now follows from the rules above rather than a word count: a compound, level-stating entry typically runs 2–6 words plus an optional parenthetical. Keep the number of tags per bullet small; when a skill can be integrated naturally into the bullet text using selective bold, prefer that over adding another tag.

### 6. Conciseness and Readability

- Keep bullets concise and easy to read.
- Remove unnecessary words while preserving meaning and impact.
- Favour clarity and natural flow over trying to include every possible keyword.
- One strong, well-crafted bullet is better than two average ones.

### 7. Multiple Bullets per Requirement (1-to-many)

- When a requirement has multiple strong pieces of evidence, create **separate bullets** rather than combining everything into one long bullet.
- Only combine evidence into one bullet if the pieces are very closely related and the result is still clear and impactful.

## C. Persistence

This step emits a single `emit_cv_bullets` tool call. The app validates it (`lib/llm/schemas.ts` →
`C4.zod`) and updates the **existing** `requirement_tailoring` rows in place
(`lib/pipeline/tailoring.ts`): your `bullet` becomes `cv_bullet`, and your `skills` become
`cv_bullet_skills` — a column of its own, so `requirement_skills` keeps meaning what the JD asked for
rather than being overwritten with your tag (CI · Split cv_bullet_skills from requirement_skills). No standalone output, export or table is produced — do not generate one.

You are given only the **Keep** rows — the evidence the candidate approved at C2, each already
carrying its ref, its requirement line, its original text and its My Skills. **Return one bullet per
ref, and echo the `ref` back unchanged**: that code is how your bullet is matched to its row. A
bullet whose ref does not match falls back to the original untransformed text, silently.

One bullet per evidence item. Where an item could yield several, write the strongest single bullet
rather than splitting — there is one `cv_bullet` per row, and a second bullet has nowhere to go.

*Historic (superseded): this step previously produced an expanded table based on the final C2 export,
adding a **CV Bullet** column and an optional **Bullet Sequence** column for split bullets, back when
it ran through the Microsoft 365 connector.*

## D. Quality & Consistency Rules

- Only use the Keep evidence supplied to you. Do not introduce new evidence that was not approved at C2.
- Maintain a consistent tone across all bullets (senior-level, strategic, action-oriented).
- Ensure bullets are **ATS-friendly** (clear keywords, structured, no excessive formatting).
- Balance **JD language** with readability. The bullet should still feel authentic to the candidate’s voice and experience.
- When in doubt between a more impressive phrasing and strict truthfulness, **choose truthfulness**.
- After writing all bullets, perform a final consistency check across the entire table (verb variety, length, impact level).

### Notes & Quality Rules

- C4 builds directly on the **final approved evidence** from C2. Any evidence that was removed during deduplication in C2 should not be used here.
- The quality of the CV bullets directly impacts both ATS scoring and the impression made on Talent Acquisition and Hiring Managers.
- Strong bullets are **specific, results-oriented, and truthful** — not generic or exaggerated.
- C4 is a critical bridge between the analytical work done in C2 and the actual CV tailoring that will happen in later steps.
- **Truthfulness**: Base the **content** of the bullet on the `Original Text` column from C2 (this is the actual evidence).
- Avoid forced rephrasing: Do not significantly alter the Original Text from C2 in order to insert skills, keywords, or to create bold inline text. Bullets should remain close to the source evidence. When in doubt, prioritise truthfulness and natural phrasing over stylistic optimisation.
- **Language alignment**: Use the `Requirement Skills` column to choose appropriate **keywords and phrasing** for the **bullet text**, where they fit naturally. The bracketed tag is named from the candidate's own register instead — §B.5, *Naming the tag*. *(Reversed 2026-08-24, [[C4 Writes CV-Grade Skill Tags]]: this line previously pointed the tag at `Requirement Skills` too.)*
